AIEL Monitor · monthly brief · 2026-10

AI and the labour market, October 2026

A monthly snapshot of AI in the labour market from the AI-Econ Lab, covering international trends with Sweden in more depth, from public data. This month: the widening gap between talk of AI and demand for AI skills.

Subscribe monthly EN · SV ← the live monitor

Employers mention AI far more often than they ask for AI skills

Much of the debate on reskilling carries a general expectation that employers will need more and more AI skills. Employers do mention AI more and more, but mentioning AI is not the same as asking for an AI skill, and the two have come apart.

Talk grows faster than skill

Floor: the job itself asks for a named AI skill. Example: a software developer required to use AI tools in coding.
Up to the ceiling: AI terms elsewhere in the advertisement, and advertisements that only say “AI” but turn out, when read by hand, to be genuine AI roles. Example: a specialist advising on EU AI regulation.
AI talk: advertisements that only say “AI” and name no skill or tool, for instance an employer describing itself as AI-driven. We do not count these as demand.

From January to September 2026, 3.6% of the 347,485 advertisements on Platsbanken mentioned AI in some way (2.4% over the whole of 2025), a share about 70 per cent higher than in the same months of 2025. We count 1.5% as AI demand: the floor, 0.6%, and a further 0.9% up to the ceiling. Strictly, we measure AI demand as a range, currently between 0.6% and 1.5%. In all, 2.4% only mention AI. Read by hand, about one in eight of them concern genuine AI roles; those 0.3 percentage points are counted in the ceiling, and the remaining 2.1% is the AI talk in grey. It has doubled since the same months of 2025, from 1.0%, and is now larger than AI demand. AI demand is also spreading beyond ICT jobs: 64% of it is in other occupations, against 50% in 2020, while the floor has stayed near 52%.

Source: JobTech / Platsbanken job advertisements, frozen v1.7 term list · distinct advertisements. Full method at ai-econlab.com/monitor/#method. 2026 covers January to September. The share of genuine AI roles among advertisements that only say “AI” (about one in eight) rests on 203 advertisements read by hand.

A mention is not a job

An advertisement that only mentions AI may do so for various reasons. The employer may be signalling that it is at the forefront, describing itself, expecting staff to use AI without naming it as a skill, or looking for applicants who are curious about AI. A small but growing share mention AI only to describe the hiring itself, such as an AI tool that conducts the first interview. Others ask applicants not to use AI when applying. In the latest version of our classification such mentions are no longer counted at all. Advertisements rarely list everything a job requires, so even the ceiling captures only the demand that is written down. How much of the bare mentions reflects a real need is hard to judge from the advertisements alone.

In the AISCAF cluster. Nordström Skans and Sokolow Romin (Uppsala University) find, in their first report with Growth Analysis (Rapport 2026:17, in Swedish), worse outcomes since 2023 for new graduates in fields where AI is especially relevant.

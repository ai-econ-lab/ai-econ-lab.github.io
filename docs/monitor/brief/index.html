AIEL Monitor · monthly brief · 2026-08

AI and the labour market, August 2026

A monthly snapshot from the AI-Econ Lab: international, with Sweden in depth, on public data. In focus this month: Barriers: what firms say stops them.

Subscribe monthly EN · SV ← the live monitor

Why so few firms use AI

Capability is no longer the obvious constraint. Frontier AI agents now finish tasks of up to 12–17 hours of human-expert work about half the time, and that length has been doubling roughly every four months. Experiments on assigned tasks find real gains: 15% more customer-support issues resolved per hour in one firm (Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, 2025); 40% less time and 18% higher quality on professional writing (Noy and Zhang, 2023); 12% more consulting tasks, 25% faster, but 19% less accurately on a task chosen outside the frontier (Dell'Acqua et al., 2026). Those are narrow tasks, set by a researcher and marked against a known standard. Reorganising complex work inside a going concern is a different problem, and the evidence that AI pays at the level of the firm is much thinner. Yet in 2025 only 20% of EU enterprises used AI, 35% in Sweden. So why does a technology that performs this well on assigned tasks see so little use? Eurostat put the question to firms across the EU.

Skills, not money

Across the EU the obstacle enterprises name most often is people, not money: a lack of relevant expertise ranks first of 8, cost only 6th, and “AI is simply not useful to us” last. Sweden puts the same reason first and the same one last, with cost in the same place; only the data and legal reasons in between swap around. Read the ranking rather than the levels, because the levels are shares of all enterprises while since 2023 the question is put only to firms that considered AI and decided against it, which Eurostat flags as a break; the chart carries them for anyone who wants them. These are therefore the obstacles of firms that engaged with the question; the larger group never entered it, and the interesting puzzle is why most never consider AI at all.

Eurostat surveys the business economy, so the public sector is absent from the chart, and in Sweden that is a large omission. Our report for the Expert Group on Public Economics found about a quarter of central government authorities and municipalities using AI against more than sixty per cent of the regions, with AI concentrated in administrative support rather than core services. The obstacle named there is competence again, with a detail the business survey cannot give: the gap is among leaders as much as among employees. Source: a self-selected Akavia member panel of 1,729 public-sector professionals, May 2024, our own processing.

The ranking travels, the levels do not

Read this as how widespread each obstacle is, not as a division of non-adopters between causes. Eurostat allows several answers, so the shares do not sum to a total and cannot be rebased onto the 65% of Swedish firms not using AI. The levels are not comparable across years, and for Sweden not across the 2023 break. Nor is any of this cut by firm size, where the variation sits: in Sweden 31% of firms with 10 to 49 employees use AI against 72% of those with 250 or more. An obstacle named is also not a benefit forgone: the survey records what firms say stopped them, not whether adopting would have paid.
